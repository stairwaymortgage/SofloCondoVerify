If you are buying a condo in South Florida, the Milestone inspection is one of the first things worth checking — and the short answer is that it is a scheduled structural checkpoint required by Florida law for most buildings three stories or taller, not a pass/fail safety grade. Knowing whether a building has completed it, is in the middle of it, or has repairs outstanding tells you what questions to bring to the association before you make an offer.

This explainer walks through what the inspection is, when it applies, and how to verify a specific building's status from official sources. The specifics — exact deadlines, which phase a building is in, what a report concluded — always come from the local building official and the association's own filings, not from any single website.

## What is a Milestone inspection?

A Milestone inspection is a structural inspection of a condominium or cooperative building performed by a licensed engineer or architect. Florida added the requirement to state law after the 2021 collapse of a residential tower in Surfside, as part of a broader effort to put structural oversight on a fixed schedule rather than leaving it to each association's discretion.

The inspection looks at the building's primary structural systems — the load-bearing elements that keep it standing. It is distinct from a routine maintenance walkthrough or a home inspection of an individual unit. The goal is to catch substantial structural deterioration early enough to address it.

Importantly, the inspection is a checkpoint, not a certification that a building is "good" or "bad." Most buildings that go through the process do so uneventfully. The value for a buyer is in knowing where a building sits in the cycle.

## Which buildings have to do it, and when?

The requirement generally applies to condominium and cooperative buildings that are **three stories or more** in height. The timing of the first inspection is tied to the building's age, with buildings nearer the coast typically facing an earlier threshold because of the harsher environment. After the first inspection, the law sets a recurring interval for future ones.

Because the deadlines are administered locally, the single most reliable step is to ask the **local building official** — the county or municipal building department — for the building's status and due date. In the tri-county area that means the building departments serving Miami-Dade, Broward, and Palm Beach and their municipalities. Dates and local procedures change, so a figure you read anywhere should be confirmed against the department that actually holds the record.

## What do Phase One and Phase Two mean?

The inspection is structured in up to two phases, and the distinction matters when you are reading a building's status:

- **Phase One** is a visual inspection by a licensed engineer or architect. If the inspector finds no signs of substantial structural deterioration, the process can conclude at Phase One. A completed Phase One with no further action is the common, unremarkable outcome.
- **Phase Two** follows only when Phase One identifies possible substantial structural deterioration. It is a more detailed evaluation that can involve testing, and it sets out the repairs the association needs to make and on what timeline.

A building described as "in Phase Two" or "repairs identified" is not a building you should rule out on that basis alone. It means there is a defined scope of work, and the sensible move is to ask the association what the scope is, what it costs, and how it is being funded.

## How can a buyer verify a building's status?

There are a few official channels, and using more than one gives you a fuller picture:

1. **The local building department.** Milestone inspection reports are filed with the local building official. That department is the authoritative source for whether an inspection is on file and what phase a building is in.
2. **The association.** Florida law requires associations to distribute a summary of the inspection report to owners. A buyer can request the inspection reports and the summary through the seller or the association.
3. **The association's financial posture.** The inspection sits alongside the reserve study and budget. A building with identified repairs and a funded plan to pay for them is in a very different position from one with repairs and no plan, even though both might read as "repairs identified" at a glance.

On a verification record, a structural filing that cannot be located should be described as **unconfirmed** rather than as a violation — an absent record is not evidence of a problem, only an absence of a filing to read. When you review a building here, that is the framing we use, and you can follow each signal back to the official source it came from.

## How does this affect financing a condo?

Structural status has become a more visible part of condo lending. Lenders and the agencies that set financing guidelines increasingly weigh a building's structural and reserve posture when deciding whether a project is eligible for a given loan type. An unresolved structural item, or a reserve shortfall tied to required repairs, can narrow the financing available in a building.

This is building-level eligibility, which is separate from your own approval as a borrower. You can be fully qualified and still find that a particular building limits which loans are realistic there. Because conventional project eligibility is not public record, the dependable way to learn where a specific building stands for conventional financing is to ask a licensed loan officer to check it directly — well before an inspection period, not during it.

## What should a buyer do before making an offer?

A short, practical checklist:

- **Look the building up first.** Use the condo verification lookup to see the structural, reserve, and approval signals on file, each dated and linked to its source.
- **Ask the local building official** for the Milestone inspection status and due date.
- **Request the inspection reports and the owner summary** from the association or seller, and read what each phase concluded.
- **Ask how any identified repairs are funded** — the budget, the reserve schedule, and whether a special assessment is contemplated.
- **Ask a licensed professional** about loan-type eligibility for that specific building before your inspection period closes.

A board that answers these questions quickly and clearly is itself a reassuring signal. One that is slow or evasive is worth noting — not as a verdict, but as a reason to dig further.

Milestone inspections exist to make a building's structural health something you can check on a schedule rather than guess at. For a buyer, the inspection status is not the end of the story; it is a starting point for the right questions. Everything specific to a building — dates, phases, findings — should be confirmed with the local building official and the association before you rely on it.
